# McKinsey 7S Framework

The McKinsey 7S Framework is a strategic management and organizational design tool created in the late 1970s by [McKinsey & Company](https://www.mckinsey.com/) consultants Tom Peters and Robert Waterman (with contributions from Richard Pascale and Anthony Athos). First published in Peters and Waterman's bestselling book [In Search of Excellence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In_Search_of_Excellence), the model posits that an organization is not just a structure of reporting lines, but a connected web of seven interdependent factors that must be fully aligned for optimal performance and successful strategy execution. [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7]

**Evidence: Weak.** Practitioner-derived from consulting experience in the
late 1970s, and never validated as a measurement model or tested for
predictive value. There is no established way to score the seven elements, no
evidence about how much each matters, and no cause-and-effect ordering among
them. Its durable contribution is the hard-versus-soft distinction and the
reminder that changing one element requires adjusting the others — a claim
that is plausible, widely observed, and not formally demonstrated. Note also
that it omits the external environment entirely, which
[burke-litwin-causal-model](burke-litwin-causal-model.md) treats as the
ultimate trigger for change.


## The Hard Elements (Tangible & Easy to Manage)

The three "Hard S’s" are the explicit, visible components of an organization. Management can easily identify them and directly manipulate them through formal plans, organizational charts, and documentation. [7, 8]

1.  Strategy: The comprehensive plan or course of action designed to allocate resources, secure a sustainable competitive advantage, and outmaneuver market rivals. [2, 8, 9]
2.  Structure: The formal hierarchy, division of activities, operational reporting lines, and organizational chart that dictate who reports to whom and how roles are grouped. [1, 2, 10]
3.  Systems: The formal and informal daily procedures, operational workflows, IT platforms, and routine processes (like budgeting, HR tracking, and performance monitoring) that keep the business running. [2, 8, 9]

## The Soft Elements (Intangible & Culture-Driven)

The four "Soft S’s" are more abstract, elusive, and rooted in the collective behavior and culture of the people. They are harder to codify or change overnight, but they ultimately determine whether the hard elements succeed. [7, 8]

1.  Shared Values (Core): Originally called "superordinate goals," these are the core beliefs, ethical standards, and cultural norms that anchor the organization. In the visual model, shared values sit at the very center because they permeate and influence all other elements. [2, 8, 9]
2.  Style: The actual behavior and operating approach of leadership and top management—not just what leaders preach in public, but how they make decisions, delegate, and interact daily. [2, 9, 11]
3.  Staff: The organization’s human talent pool, focusing on general demographics, career development, recruitment pipelines, and overall talent management philosophies. [2, 8]
4.  Skills: The distinctive, core capabilities, competencies, and technical expertise possessed by the workforce and the enterprise as a whole. [1, 2, 8]

## Key Takeaways for Application

- Interconnectedness: There is no distinct hierarchy among the seven factors; a modification or shift in any single "S" triggers a ripple effect that requires adjustments in the remaining six to preserve harmony. [9, 10, 12, 13]
- The Root Cause of Failure: Most failed organizational change initiatives happen because leaders focus exclusively on pulling the "hard" levers (like altering the structure or announcing a new strategy) while ignoring the "soft" levers (like changing leadership style or addressing misaligned shared values). [7]
